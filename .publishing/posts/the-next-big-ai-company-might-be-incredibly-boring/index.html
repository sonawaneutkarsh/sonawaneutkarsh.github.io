the next big ai company might be incredibly boring.

2026-11-08

this is a follow-up to my earlier post on why small language models could make sense for businesses. i've been thinking more about where AI actually creates value, and honestly, a lot of the best opportunities might be incredibly boring.

one way to look at technological progress is that we keep finding ways to do useful things with less human effort. the wheel made moving heavy things easier. mechanization changed manufacturing. computers automated calculations and paperwork. software turned entire administrative processes into buttons.

now we have AI, and the interesting part isn't just that a model can write an essay or generate code. it's that you can describe a task in ordinary language and have software attempt it. you don't necessarily need to know how to program to start experimenting. that lowers the barrier to building things, even if making them reliable still requires real engineering.

most AI conversation revolves around frontier models, flashy demos, and the next general-purpose assistant. but go look at what a normal business spends its time doing. someone copies information from PDFs into spreadsheets. someone reviews invoices. someone checks insurance forms, triages support requests, tracks shipments, or follows up on leads. these are repetitive workflows, and companies spend real time and money on them.

take lead generation. a business might spend hours identifying companies that fit its target customer, finding the relevant decision-makers, researching public contact information, and checking whether the data is usable. AI can help with the research, filtering, and repetitive steps. the difficult part is making sure the information is accurate and actually useful.

that's where smaller, specialized models get interesting to me. for a narrow task with a clear input and output, you might not need the biggest model available. an SLM with good data, evaluation, and a carefully designed workflow could be cheaper, faster, or easier to run privately. could is important here: a small model that makes costly mistakes isn't cheaper in the end.

i don't think this means companies suddenly don't need developers. some routine coding gets easier, but integration, reliability, security, data quality, and knowing what a business actually needs become more important. a polished demo is not the same thing as a production system.

i've also been watching consumer-facing assistants try to automate everyday tasks. hark's pizza experiment in october 2026 was a funny example: people could ask an assistant to order a pizza as part of a promotion. i even got a free pizza :D

now imagine automating repetitive tasks inside logistics companies, dental offices, or accounting firms. making these systems reliable, and handling edge cases and approvals, is where a lot of the work is.

that leads to another interesting thought. if everyone has access to powerful models, then the model itself might not be the defensible part of the company. maybe it's knowing one industry's workflows better than anyone else. the integrations, data, distribution, customer relationships, and trust are harder to copy than an API call.

the next big AI startup might not look like a breakthrough in artificial intelligence.

it might look like an accounting company that somehow gets everything done on time, at half the cost.
